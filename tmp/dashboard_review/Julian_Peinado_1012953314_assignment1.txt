Q1 Exploratory data analysis 
How does developer pay vary with work arrangement and education? In the selected sample, remote respondents earn 
more at both the mean and median, while US/Canada degree groups differ in average pay. The question is how robust 
these patterns are to salary extremes and statistical uncertainty. 
Study Population and Sample Selection Criteria 
To focus the study on workforce participation, I exclude DevType = “Retired” and recorded Age >80 before all analyses. 
These researcher-defined scope decisions reflect engineering judgment, not data cleaning or evidence that older people 
are outliers; age 80 remains eligible. Of 20,407 original rows, 2 retired and 93 older respondents are excluded, with no 
overlap, leaving 20,312. Both retired records also say Employed, so the explicit DevType classification governs. 
Retirement exclusion does not establish active employment: 321 students and 58 respondents marked Not employed 
remain. Supplementary paid-work responses support self-reported participation, not independent verification. The 10 
missing ages remain outside the age plot, so the ceiling cannot be verified for every respondent. 
Within this population, satisfaction analysis omits 3,428 missing ratings and one value of 60, using a working scale of 0–
10. Each row has one supplied job category. All eligible salary extremes remain in the primary analyses. 
 
Figures 1–2 show the lowest two and highest three means; the notebook covers all 31 retained titles. Senior executives lead 
satisfaction (8.12/10, n=168). Financial analysts/engineers average $501,248 (n=46), against a median of $87,248.50: an immediate 
warning that a high mean can reflect a small upper tail. 
 
Figure 3 retains salaries from $102 to $33.55 million on a log axis. Three recorded ages below 15 remain flagged in the notebook; their 
validity and the age-cleaning history are uncertain. These diagnostics cause no further salary exclusions.

Q2(c): Bootstrap analysis 
Bootstrapping offers a second view of uncertainty. Within each group, I resample with replacement, retaining 6,888 
Remote and 7,776 Hybrid salaries per draw. With 10,000 replications, seed 16242026 and separate named NumPy 
streams, the mean SEs are $5,362.02 and $3,890.15; the difference SE is $6,605.63. These describe uncertainty in means, 
not dispersion between individual salaries.

Q2(d): T-test using bootstrapped data 
The required pooled t-test on two arrays of 10,000 unshifted bootstrap means gives t=258.685, df=19,998 and p below 
floating-point resolution. It rejects, but simulations reuse the same respondents. The nominal SE falls as 1/√B; more 
replications can reduce p without adding population evidence. 
For a separate null test, each group is shifted to the combined mean, preserving shape and variance. Resamples retain 
original sizes and recalculate the pooled SE. Comparing |T₀*|=|(Δ*−Δ̂)/SE*| with |2.63508| yields 50 extreme draws: 
p=(50+1)/10,001=0.005099. This rejects under an approximate null calibration; empirical tails and simulation error affect 
the result.

Q2(f): Median comparison 
Does the advantage persist for a typical salary? Remote and Hybrid medians are $90,491 and $77,730, a gap of $12,761. 
Mood’s test counts values above versus at/below the pooled median of $81,870; ties (11 Remote, 5 Hybrid) go below. 
With Yates correction, χ²(1)=109.1245, p=1.524e-25, rejecting equal medians at 0.05. 
I bootstrap the median difference 10,000 times at original group sizes. Shifting each group to the pooled median is 
equivalent to subtracting the observed gap from each simulated gap. There are 0 extreme absolute null gaps; plus-one 
p=9.999e-05. The 95% Monte Carlo upper bound is 0.0003688. A zero exceedance count identifies the simulation floor, 
not a precise population tail probability. 
The two methods agree on the direction of the result despite measuring evidence differently. Mood’s test uses threshold 
counts and a chi-square approximation; the bootstrap uses dollar-valued gaps and empirical distributions. Because 
medians are less sensitive to extremes, this agreement shows that the remote advantage is also present at the center of 
these samples.

Q3(b): Original-data ANOVA 
To test the overall pattern, H₀: μB=μM=μP is compared with the alternative that at least one mean differs. ANOVA gives 
F(2, 4,313)=18.7362, p=7.909e-09. The exact reference assumes independence, normal errors and equal variances. A 
variance ratio of 2.96 and Levene p=1.626e-05 warrant caution; Welch ANOVA also rejects, F(2, 682.27)=12.9764, 
p=2.944e-06. Neither omnibus result identifies significant pairs.

Q3(c): Bootstrap means and pairwise differences 
The bootstrap reveals why the degree means have unequal precision. Across 10,000 resamples of sizes 2,954/1,067/295 
(B/M/P), mean SEs are $2,100.56/$5,000.74/$11,546.91. The smaller Professional group is much less precise. Separate 
streams use seed 16242026; shared group draws preserve dependence between contrasts. Observed B−M, B−P and M−P 
gaps are −$22,122.51, −$38,585.18 and −$16,462.67. 
 
Figures 7–9 show degree means, pairwise differences and standardized differences as probability densities. Each contrast is centered 
and scaled using its own bootstrap mean and SD. These plots describe uncertainty; they are neither null distributions nor formal post-
hoc tests. 
Q3(d): ANOVA using bootstrapped data 
ANOVA on the three arrays of 10,000 unshifted bootstrap means gives F(2, 29,997)=69,977.6132; its p-value underflows 
numerically. Although it rejects, increasing B adds simulations rather than respondents. As in Q2, this calculation supplies 
no independent confirmation and identifies no significant pair. 
For a separate test under equal means, I shift each degree group to the combined mean and resample at its original size, 
preserving empirical variances and shapes. None of 10,000 null F* values reaches the observed statistic: plus -one 
p=1/10,001=0.000100. This approximate calibration also rejects, but the simulation floor is not a precise tail estimate. No 
formal post-hoc tests were performed. 
Conclusions and limitations 
The evidence tells a consistent but qualified story: remote respondents have higher mean and median pay, and 
US/Canada degree groups differ overall. Median and sensitivity results show that the remote advantage is not solely an 
extreme-salary phenomenon; degree-tail sensitivity and unequal bootstrap precision qualify the education ranking. 
These associations cannot isolate effects of work mode or education from country, occupation and experience. The 
selected sample is not a verified employed-only population. Self-report, survey selection and uncertain independence 
limit generalization; bootstrapping cannot repair them. Eight identical rows remain because respondent identities are 
unverified, and no overall multiplicity correction was applied.
